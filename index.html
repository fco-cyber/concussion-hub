Definition, scope and epidemiology

Concussion is a physiological brain injury — a disturbance of function, not primarily of structure. It is diagnosed clinically, recovers in most people within days to weeks, and is one of the few brain injuries where what you do in the first two weeks measurably changes the outcome.

Current consensus definition — Amsterdam 2023

Sport-related concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a direct blow to the head, neck or body resulting in an impulsive force being transmitted to the brain. It initiates a neurotransmitter and metabolic cascade, with possible axonal injury, blood-flow change and inflammation. Signs and symptoms may appear immediately or evolve over minutes to hours, commonly resolve within days but may be prolonged. No abnormality is seen on standard structural neuroimaging. Loss of consciousness may or may not occur.1

In plain language

A concussion is a brain injury caused by a knock to the head or to the body that jolts the brain. Scans are usually normal — that does not mean nothing happened. It means the injury is to how brain cells are working, not to their structure, and that is exactly why it recovers.

Most people feel substantially better within 1–4 weeks. Recovery is helped by getting back to gentle activity early, sleeping well, and easing back into work or school in steps rather than all at once.

The three rules that matter on the field

  1. If in doubt, sit them out. A suspected concussion is removed immediately and does not return the same day — at any level, any age.
  2. You do not need to be knocked out. Fewer than 1 in 10 concussions involve loss of consciousness.
  3. Symptoms can be delayed. Re-check at 15 minutes, after the game, and the next morning.

Where concussions happen

Rates are highest in rugby, American football, ice hockey and wrestling among males, and in soccer, rugby, basketball and lacrosse among females. Games carry roughly 2 more concussions per 10,000 exposures than practice, collegiate rates exceed high-school rates, and — in nearly every sex-comparable sport — female athletes have higher rates than males.31 Outside sport, falls (older adults and young children), road traffic and assault dominate; these patients are older, more symptomatic and more likely to have persisting problems than the sporting cohorts most guidelines were built on.21

Pathophysiology: the neurometabolic cascade

Biomechanical force stretches axons and cell membranes. Ion channels open indiscriminately, glutamate floods the synapse, and the brain must spend enormous energy to restore ionic balance — at exactly the moment cerebral blood flow falls. This energy-supply mismatch is the central event, and it explains almost every clinical feature of concussion.10,11

Stage by stage

1. Immediate — mechanoporation, ionic flux and glutamate release (seconds to minutes)

Membrane stretch opens voltage-gated and mechanosensitive channels. Potassium pours out of neurons; calcium pours in. Indiscriminate glutamate release activates NMDA receptors, amplifying the ionic shift. The neuron depolarises abruptly and then is functionally silenced — this is the substrate for the immediate "lights are on but nobody's home" presentation, amnesia, and brief LOC.10,11,12

Clinical link Diffuse depolarisation followed by spreading suppression maps onto the migraine phenotype seen acutely (headache, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea) — one reason migraine-specific management works in this group.

2. Hours — hyperglycolysis and the energy crisis

Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pumps work overtime to restore gradients, driving a surge in glucose consumption. Simultaneously, cerebral blood flow drops — demonstrated directly in paediatric athletes, where CBF fell to ~38 vs 48 mL/100 g/min in controls and had normalised in only 27% at 14 days and 64% beyond 30 days.13 Demand up, supply down: the mismatch is the injury.

3. Days to weeks — metabolic depression, calcium and mitochondria

Intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulates in mitochondria, impairing oxidative metabolism. Glucose utilisation falls below baseline for days to weeks. N-acetylaspartate — a marker of neuronal metabolic health — decreases and recovers slowly, and its trajectory can lag symptom resolution.15,10 This is the physiological basis for clinical recovery preceding physiological recovery, and the reason exercise tolerance testing adds information a symptom score cannot.

4. Axonal injury, neuroinflammation and glymphatic failure

Rotational strain causes cytoskeletal misalignment, impaired axonal transport and, in some axons, secondary axotomy — visible on diffusion imaging but not on CT or standard MRI. Microglial and astrocytic activation follows. Newer work implicates the glymphatic/perivascular clearance system: altered aquaporin-4 polarisation, increased perivascular space burden and impaired solute clearance are found after mTBI and correlate with persistent cognitive symptoms.16 In former American football players, perivascular space volume scaled with cumulative rotational head impact exposure and with worse cognition.28

5. Autonomic and cerebrovascular dysregulation — why exercise intolerance happens

Concussion disrupts autonomic control of cerebral blood flow and ventilation. During exercise, ventilation is inappropriately low for workload, arterial CO₂ rises, cerebral blood flow increases out of proportion to intensity, and symptoms are provoked at a reproducible heart rate — the heart rate threshold (HRt).36 This is measurable (Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test), treatable (sub-threshold aerobic exercise), and gives concussion care an objective physiological endpoint.

Why it matters clinically Exercise intolerance is present in over 80% of adults presenting with persisting symptoms39 and predicts delayed recovery in adolescents — a Δ HR (resting → symptom threshold) ≤50 bpm was 73% sensitive and 78% specific for recovery beyond 30 days under a rest-based standard of care.37

What is actually happening in there

The impact stretches brain cells and makes them leak. Putting that right takes a large amount of energy — and at exactly the same moment, blood flow to the brain drops. So demand goes up while supply goes down.

That mismatch is the injury, and it explains almost everything you feel. Thinking hard, bright supermarkets, screens and exercise all raise the brain's energy demand, which is why they bring symptoms on. It also explains why a second knock during this window is far more dangerous than the first.

The good news is that this is a functional problem, not damaged tissue — which is precisely why it recovers, and why gently and progressively raising the demand again is how you retrain it.

The clinically useful summary

Concussion is an energy crisis with an autonomic tail. That single sentence explains symptom provocation by exertion, the vulnerability to a second injury, the failure of prolonged rest (which deconditions and does nothing for the autonomic problem), and the success of graded sub-threshold aerobic exercise.

Biomechanics, anatomy and the cervical contribution

Rotational head motion is the dominant contributor to injury risk because the brain resists shear far less than compression, and because its tethering (falx, tentorium, brainstem, vasculature) concentrates strain at the grey–white junction, corpus callosum, midbrain and — critically — the depths of cortical sulci.17 Instrumented mouthguards have made large-scale in-vivo measurement feasible, but a definitive concussion threshold remains elusive: the same impact concusses one athlete and not another.17,35

Practical framing

Red flags and emergency management

Concussion is a diagnosis of exclusion at the pitch-side and in the emergency department. The first question is never "is this a concussion?" — it is "is this something that will kill or disable this person in the next few hours?"

⚠ Call emergency services / transfer immediately if ANY of these are present
  • Neck pain or tenderness — immobilise, assume cervical spine injury
  • Double vision
  • Weakness or tingling/burning in more than one arm, or in the legs
  • Severe or increasing headache
  • Seizure or convulsion
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Deteriorating conscious state
  • Vomiting (especially repeated)
  • Increasingly restless, agitated or combative
  • GCS < 15
  • Visible deformity of the skull

The 11 Concussion Recognition Tool 6 (CRT6) red flags, Amsterdam 20231 — and although it is not on the list, an escalating concern from a bystander that something is badly wrong deserves the same response.

Additional clinical red flags beyond the CRT6

  • Anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy, or known bleeding disorder — lower the threshold for imaging substantially, and consider delayed haemorrhage (24–48 h) even after a normal initial CT.
  • Age >65 with any mechanism, or age >80 with GCS 15 and no other features — high rate of significant intracranial injury.
  • Suspected non-accidental injury in a child; mechanism inconsistent with injury; delayed presentation.
  • Post-traumatic amnesia >30 minutes, GCS <15 at 2 hours, suspected open or depressed fracture, any sign of basal skull fracture (haemotympanum, panda eyes, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea, Battle's sign), ≥2 vomiting episodes, dangerous mechanism — Canadian CT Head Rule criteria.32
  • Thunderclap onset, Horner's syndrome, pulsatile tinnitus, or neck pain with neurological signs — consider cervical arterial dissection, particularly after rotational neck trauma.
  • Progressive focal deficit, papilloedema, or persistent vomiting days after injury — exclude subdural haematoma and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.
Cervical spine first

Neck pain or midline tenderness after a concussive mechanism means the cervical spine is cleared before concussion assessment proceeds. Do not perform the coordination, balance or vestibulo-ocular components of a SCAT6 in a patient with an uncleared cervical spine.

Go to an emergency department right away if you notice

Worsening or very severe headache, repeated vomiting, one pupil larger than the other, seizures, weakness or numbness in an arm or leg, slurred speech, unusual drowsiness or difficulty waking, confusion that is getting worse, or behaviour that is out of character and escalating.

You do not need to wake someone every hour. Once assessed and cleared, normal sleep is beneficial. Someone should be nearby for the first 24 hours and able to check on them.

Sideline recognition — CRT6 and immediate removal

The Concussion Recognition Tool 6 is written for non-medical users: coaches, teachers, parents, teammates. It identifies suspicion, not diagnosis. Anyone with suspected concussion is removed from play immediately and does not return the same day.1

Visible signs — any one of these warrants removal

  • Lying motionless, or slow to get up
  • Loss of consciousness or unresponsiveness
  • Falling unprotected to the surface
  • Blank or vacant stare
  • Balance difficulties, stumbling, uncoordinated movement
  • Disorientation or confusion; inability to respond appropriately
  • Facial injury after head trauma
  • Seizure, convulsion, or tonic posturing
  • High-risk mechanism (head-to-head, elbow to head, unprotected fall)
  • Behaviour change noted by teammates or coaches

Maddocks questions — sport-specific orientation

Failure of any question raises suspicion. Standard orientation questions (name, date) are not sensitive enough in sport.

  1. What venue are we at today?
  2. Which half is it now?
  3. Who scored last in this match?
  4. What team did you play last week/game?
  5. Did your team win the last game?
Coach / parent action card
  1. Recognise — any visible sign, any red flag, any report of symptoms.
  2. Remove — off the field, out of the game and out of training. Do not leave alone.
  3. Refer — to a healthcare professional the same day if possible.
  4. Rest & restart — 24–48 h relative rest, then begin light activity. Do not prescribe a dark room for a week.
  5. Return — only through the graded strategy, and only with medical clearance before contact.
Sideline pitfalls with real consequences
  • Delayed onset is common. A normal 5-minute sideline assessment does not exclude concussion. Re-assess at 15 minutes, post-game, and next day.
  • Athletes under-report. Objective, involuntary measures (vestibulo-ocular responses, tandem gait) are harder to mask than symptom scales.2
  • Fatigue does not invalidate the tools. SCAT6 and mVOMS performance was unchanged immediately and 20 minutes after intense training in high-school athletes; tandem gait actually improved (learning effect).8
  • Baseline testing is not required. Incorporating preseason baselines did not significantly improve diagnostic accuracy for acute concussion in over 3,900 collegiate athletes, and baseline test–retest reliability was poor to moderate.6

Acute assessment — SCAT6 and Child SCAT6

The SCAT6 is for healthcare professionals, ages 13+ (Child SCAT6: ages 8–12). Its diagnostic power is greatest within 72 hours of injury, diminishes progressively to 7 days, and it has limited utility as a return-to-play tool beyond 7 days.3

A faster, better-performing acute screen — mSIT Plus

Analysis of 59,901 athletes and cadets in the NCAA-DoD CARE Consortium found a 6-item symptom index (headache, pressure in head, don't feel right, sensitivity to light, dizziness, sensitivity to noise; 0–36) outperformed the full 22-item SCAT severity score: AUC 0.94 vs 0.88, sensitivity 87% vs 85%, specificity 88% vs 76%. Adding a modified VOMS raised specificity to 92%. Every other test in the battery — SAC, mBESS, ImPACT, King-Devick, reaction time — was non-additive once these two were used. Proposed cut-offs: mSIT ≥2 and mVOMS ≥2, completed in 2–3 minutes.7

Caveat This is single-consortium evidence and not yet in consensus guidance; use it to triage and prioritise, not to replace a documented full assessment where one is required.

Documented limitations you should state in your notes

  • Temporal stability is limited — test–retest reliability is poor to moderate across SCAT components (r = 0.23–0.52; ICC 0.36–0.68).6 Do not over-interpret small serial changes.
  • Evidence is heavily North American, adult and male-weighted; data are scant in pre-adolescents, women, para athletes, and culturally or linguistically diverse populations.3
  • Suboptimal effort is common in youth populations and in athletes motivated to return.2,5
  • A normal SCAT6 does not exclude concussion. The diagnosis is clinical.

Clinical profiles — the organising framework

Rather than treating "concussion", identify which profiles are driving the presentation. Most patients have two or three, with one dominant. This determines the rehabilitation prescription.

Vestibular
Dizziness, motion sensitivity, symptoms in busy environments, fogginess in crowds. VOMS VOR/VMS positive.
Treat: vestibular rehabilitation — habituation, gaze stabilisation, dynamic balance; canalith repositioning if BPPV.
Ocular-motor
Difficulty reading, screen intolerance, frontal headache with near work, losing place on the page. NPC break >6 cm, saccade/pursuit provocation.
Treat: oculomotor and vergence therapy, near-work pacing, optometric referral if refractory.
Cognitive / fatigue
Mental fog, slowed processing, disproportionate fatigue, symptoms worsen through the day.
Treat: activity pacing, cognitive load graded exposure, sleep stabilisation, structured return to learn/work.
Post-traumatic migraine / headache
Throbbing headache, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea. Personal or family migraine history is common.
Treat: headache hygiene, trigger management, targeted pharmacotherapy, avoid medication overuse.
Cervical
Neck pain, occipital headache, cervicogenic dizziness, reduced ROM, joint position error.

Anxiety / mood
Rumination, hypervigilance to symptoms, avoidance, low mood, fear of re-injury or of activity.

A seventh — autonomic / physiological (exercise intolerance, orthostatic intolerance, poor heart-rate recovery) — cuts across all of them and is the one most directly modifiable by sub-threshold aerobic exercise.36,39

Interpretation — what the numbers actually support
  • Cut-off: VOMS total ≥4, any single item ≥2 above baseline, or NPC break ≥5 cm, optimises acute identification. AUC for VOMS total ≈ 0.85.4,6
  • Modified VOMS (mVOMS) — smooth pursuit, horizontal saccades, horizontal VOR, visual motion sensitivity — gives an identical AUC to the full VOMS with four items instead of seven.4
  • Added to a SCAT, VOMS improves diagnostic AUC by up to 4–8%.4,33
  • NPC distance alone is a weak discriminator (d = 0.79 vs 2.39–2.45 for other items) — it identifies a treatable impairment rather than the injury.4,9
  • False positives ~22% in systematic review; reported sensitivity 58–96%, specificity 46–92% across heterogeneous protocols. It is a screening tool within a multimodal assessment, never a standalone test.2,34
  • A positive VOMS predicts delayed recovery and identifies patients who respond to targeted vestibular/oculomotor rehabilitation.34
  • In children: long-term test–retest agreement is poor and symptom provocation decreases with age — consider annual baselines in paediatric programmes if using VOMS routinely. The VOMS-Child uses binary symptom reporting for ages 5–9.2,5

Cervical spine examination — the minimum dataset

  1. Screen for serious pathology — Canadian C-Spine Rule, upper cervical instability tests (alar ligament, transverse ligament, Sharp-Purser) where indicated, vertebrobasilar insufficiency symptoms (5 Ds and 3 Ns).
  2. Active and passive ROM in all six directions, noting pain, restriction and symptom reproduction.
  3. Segmental palpation — C0–C3 especially; reproduction of the patient's habitual headache is diagnostically important for cervicogenic headache.
  4. Cervical flexion-rotation test — <32° rotation is suggestive of C1–C2 dysfunction, common in cervicogenic headache.
  5. Joint position error — laser-pointer head relocation to neutral from 50° rotation; error >4.5° indicates proprioceptive deficit.
  6. Deep neck flexor endurance (cranio-cervical flexion test) and scapular control — commonly deficient and directly trainable.
  7. Cervical torsion test — trunk rotated under fixed head; symptom provocation implicates cervical rather than vestibular origin.
  8. Dix–Hallpike / supine roll to exclude BPPV — treatable in one visit with canalith repositioning, and frequently missed.

Balance testing

Modified BESS: three stances (double leg, single leg on non-dominant, tandem) on a firm surface, hands on hips, eyes closed, 20 seconds each. Errors: hands off hips, eyes open, step/stumble/fall, hip abduction >30°, forefoot or heel lifted, out of position >5 s. Maximum 10 errors per stance, 30 total.

Evidence caveat mBESS has small acute effect sizes (d ≈ 0.19) and poor standalone discriminative utility (AUC 0.48–0.59).4,33 Timed tandem gait (single and dual-task) is the better sensorimotor measure and one of the three SCOAT6 components with demonstrated utility.9 Note that both tandem gait measures improve with repeated administration and after exercise — a learning effect, not recovery.8

Imaging and fluid biomarkers

The one-line summary

Imaging and biomarkers are used to exclude structural injury, not to diagnose or clear concussion. Amsterdam 2023 concluded that fluid and imaging biomarkers currently have limited clinical utility in diagnosing SRC or assessing recovery from it.1,5

Structural imaging

  • CT head — the test for surgically significant intracranial injury. Normal in the overwhelming majority of concussion. Use a validated decision rule (Canadian CT Head Rule: sensitivity 93–100%, specificity 28–65%) rather than clinical gestalt.32
  • MRI — indicated for persistent or progressive deficits, atypical course, or suspicion of a non-concussive lesion. Susceptibility-weighted imaging detects microhaemorrhage; standard sequences are normal in concussion by definition.
  • Advanced modalities (DTI, functional MRI, MR spectroscopy, arterial spin labelling, perivascular space quantification) — research tools. They demonstrate real group-level abnormalities in white matter integrity, connectivity, NAA and cerebral blood flow, but lack the individual-level accuracy for clinical decisions.13,16,28

Three things clinicians get wrong about TBI biomarkers
  1. They answer "is there a bleed?", not "is this a concussion?" A negative GFAP/UCH-L1 excludes CT-visible injury with near-perfect sensitivity — the patient may still be concussed.
  2. Timing is everything. S100B requires sampling within 3 hours; GFAP/UCH-L1 within ~12 hours. Late samples are uninterpretable.
  3. Specificity is low and confounded. Age >80 significantly reduces specificity for all three; extracranial fractures inflate S100B and UCH-L1; haemolysis ≥400 mg/L haemoglobin markedly elevates UCH-L1; neurodegenerative disease elevates GFAP. Age-adjusted thresholds improve specificity.29

Why symptoms can persist without brain damage

Headaches, dizziness and fatigue after a concussion often come from the neck, the balance system in the inner ear, the eye-movement system, disrupted sleep, or from stress and worry about the injury. These are all treatable — and none of them mean the brain is damaged. Finding which ones apply to you is the point of a proper assessment.

The first 24–48 hours

The single biggest change in concussion care this decade

Strict rest until symptoms resolve is not effective and may be harmful. A meta-analysis of 46 studies (34 at low/acceptable risk of bias) found that physical activity and prescribed exercise shortened recovery by a mean of 4.64 days (95% CI −6.69 to −2.59). Early return to light physical activity in the first 2 days, prescribed sub-threshold aerobic exercise from days 2–14, and reduced screen use in the first 2 days all safely facilitate recovery. Sleep disturbance impairs it.22

Sleep and screens — what the evidence actually says

  • Sleep: disturbed sleep is consistently associated with slower recovery. Protect it actively — consistent schedule, limit daytime napping to short early naps, address pain and anxiety, avoid sedative-hypnotics as first line, consider CBT-I if insomnia persists beyond 2–4 weeks.22
  • Screens: the evidence supports reduced screen use during the first ~48 hours, not prolonged screen abstinence. Extended screen bans remove social contact and school engagement, which harms mood and recovery.22,5
  • Cognitive activity: graded, tolerable cognitive load is appropriate from day 2. "Cognitive rest" is a pacing strategy, not an abstinence rule.

What the first 48 hours look like for a player

  • You are out for the day. No exceptions, no "I'll see how I feel in the second half".
  • Someone stays with you for the first 24 hours. No driving, no alcohol, no gym.
  • Two quiet days — then you start moving. Relative rest means easy walking and normal daily life, not a dark room. From day 2, gentle aerobic work is treatment.
  • Tell the truth on the symptom check. Under-reporting to get back sooner is the single most reliable way to end up out for months instead of weeks.
  • Expect to feel off during school or work. That is normal and manageable with adjustments — it is not a reason to sit in a dark room.

Your first two days, practically

  • Take it easy — but you do not need to lie in a dark room. Gentle walking is good for you.
  • Cut back on screens for the first couple of days, then build back up.
  • Sleep normally. You do not need to be woken through the night once you've been assessed.
  • Paracetamol/acetaminophen for headache is fine. Avoid alcohol. Don't drive until you're cleared.
  • Stay with someone, or have someone check in on you, for the first 24 hours.
  • From day 2, start moving. A 20-minute walk or gentle bike that keeps your symptoms mild is treatment, not a risk.

Progression rule

Move to the next step when the current one is tolerated. A mild, brief increase in symptoms (≤2 points on a 0–10 scale, settling within about an hour) is acceptable and does not mean going backwards. A larger or sustained increase means stepping back one level for 24 hours, then trying again — it is a pacing problem, not a setback.

Academic and workplace adjustments worth requesting

  • Scheduled rest breaks (e.g. 10 min per hour) in a quiet space
  • Reduced screen brightness; larger fonts; blue-light and glare reduction
  • Extra time for assessments; deferred or reduced examination load
  • Printed or pre-prepared notes to reduce simultaneous listening and writing
  • Permission to wear sunglasses / a cap indoors, or ear defenders in noisy environments
  • Avoid noisy environments — cafeterias, assemblies, open-plan hot-desking

Do not let adjustments become permanent

Prolonged accommodation can entrench avoidance and disability beliefs, particularly in adolescents and in workers with compensation involvement. Set an explicit review date at the outset and taper adjustments actively.

For students, parents and employers

Missing school or work entirely is usually worse than attending part-time with adjustments. Social contact, routine and a sense of progress all support recovery. Aim to get back in the building early, even if the day is short.

Clearance is a physiological judgement, not a symptom checklist

Before clearing into the contact phase (step 4 onward), confirm: (a) symptoms at pre-injury baseline at rest and with exertion; (b) normal exercise tolerance — able to reach ≥90% of age-predicted maximum heart rate without symptom exacerbation on a Buffalo Concussion Treadmill/Bike Test; (c) normal cervical, vestibulo-ocular and balance examination; (d) return to learn/work completed; (e) no reliance on medication that could mask symptoms. Clinical recovery precedes physiological recovery — exercise testing is what closes that gap.36,38

Post-concussion musculoskeletal injury risk — an underrated consequence

Athletes carry elevated musculoskeletal injury risk for months after clinical recovery from concussion. Two interventions have randomised evidence:

  • Early aerobic exercise treatment — 5.3% of adolescents randomised to sub-threshold aerobic exercise sustained an MSK injury within ~3.5 months of recovery vs 24% randomised to stretching; stretching participants were 6.4× more likely to be injured (95% CI 1.14–36.05).40
  • An 8-week neuromuscular training programme after clearance — hazard of subsequent injury over the following year was 3.56× higher (95% CI 1.11–11.49) in standard care vs the training group.45
The athlete's version of the six steps
  1. Normal day. Get up, move about, don't lie in the dark. Mild symptoms are OK.
  2. Get your heart rate up. Walk, then jog or spin. Nothing that could knock your head.
  3. Your sport, on your own. Running, skating, ball work. Still no contact, no heading, no risk of a bang.
  4. Train with the team — no contact. Full intensity drills, weights back in.
  5. Full contact training.
  6. Play — with written medical clearance.

What "return to sport" means for you

Six stages, at least a day each, moving from ordinary daily activity through exercise to full competition. You do not have to feel perfect to start — you have to feel perfect before the contact stages.

The one non-negotiable: nothing with a risk of another head knock until your symptoms have settled and a healthcare professional has cleared you. The brain is more vulnerable to a second injury during recovery, and that is the situation worth avoiding at all costs.

Exercise as treatment — the strongest intervention evidence in concussion

Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT)

A graded, symptom-limited treadmill test that identifies the heart rate threshold (HRt) at which symptoms are provoked, converting a subjective complaint into a prescribable number.36,38

Protocol

  1. Record resting HR, blood pressure and baseline symptom rating (0–10 visual analogue scale).
  2. Treadmill (Balke-based): fixed comfortable walking speed (typically 3.2–3.6 mph, set by height/fitness), 0% incline for the first minute, then incline increases 1° per minute for 15 minutes; thereafter incline is fixed and speed increases 0.4 mph per minute.
  3. Record HR, rating of perceived exertion (Borg 6–20) and symptom VAS every minute.
  4. Terminate at: a ≥3-point rise in symptom VAS from baseline (symptom-limited — record HR as HRt), OR voluntary exhaustion (RPE >17), OR ≥90% of age-predicted maximum HR (220−age) achieved without symptom provocation (= exercise tolerant).
  5. Monitor recovery HR for 2 minutes.

Buffalo Concussion Bike Test (BCBT) is an equivalent alternative — HR at symptom exacerbation is statistically equivalent to the BCTT (137 ± 28 vs 135 ± 25 bpm) and it is preferable where mobility, lower-limb injury or space is limiting.41

Prescribing the exercise

The prescription used in the positive trials
  • Intensity: ~80–90% of the heart rate at which symptoms were provoked (HRt). Where no test is available, a progressive percentage of age-predicted HRmax is a validated alternative.53
  • Duration: 20 minutes per session (up to 30 in adult protocols).
  • Frequency: daily, or 5 days per week.
  • Mode: anything continuous and non-contact — walking, stationary bike, elliptical, light jogging.
  • Progression: retest weekly; re-set the target HR upward as tolerance improves.
  • Rule: stop the session if symptoms rise more than mildly. Exercising below the threshold is the active ingredient.

Nuances the headline results hide

  • Adherence, not randomisation, drives benefit. In one RCT, group assignment produced no difference, but participants achieving ≥100 min/week had significantly lower symptom severity at one month.46 Prescribe a dose and monitor it.
  • Sex differences may matter. In a pilot RCT, females prescribed high-volume exercise appeared to recover more slowly than those on low volume (p = 0.058), and 88% of exercise-intolerant females had to stop a bout early versus 17% of males. Titrate conservatively in female athletes.47
  • High mood-symptom burden is the group that benefits most. In pooled secondary analysis of two RCTs, the intervention hazard ratio for developing PPCS was 0.29 (95% CI 0.12–0.68) in adolescents with a high burden of new emotional symptoms, versus a non-significant 0.77 in those with low burden.48
  • Not every trial is positive. Several smaller RCTs found no between-group difference in days to recovery, and a systematic review of 9 RCTs (n=346) concluded the evidence is "promising" rather than definitive.54 The consistent finding across all trials is that early exercise does not harm and does not prolong recovery.
  • Generalisability: most trials are in adolescent athletes. Adult, non-athlete and community populations are less studied, though supervised early aerobic exercise also improved outcomes over standard advice in adults.55
How to do this yourself, safely
  1. Pick something continuous and with no risk of a knock — brisk walking, a stationary bike, a cross-trainer.
  2. Work at a level where you could hold a conversation but wouldn't want to sing. If you have been given a target heart rate, use it.
  3. 20 minutes a day, most days.
  4. Mild symptoms during or just after are expected and fine. If symptoms climb clearly and stay up, you went too hard — ease back tomorrow, don't stop altogether.
  5. Build up week by week. Getting your exercise tolerance back is the recovery.

Building the programme

Vestibular rehabilitation
  • Screen and treat BPPV first — Dix–Hallpike / supine roll, then Epley or appropriate repositioning. Often a single-visit fix that is missed for months.
  • Gaze stabilisation (adaptation) — VOR×1 and VOR×2, horizontal then vertical, seated → standing → walking; start at 1–2 min ×3–5 daily and progress by duration, background complexity and base of support.
  • Habituation — repeated, controlled exposure to the specific movements that provoke symptoms; graded by frequency, not intensity.
  • Sensory reweighting / balance — narrow base, foam surface, eyes closed, head movement, dual-task; progress to sport-specific.
  • Dose: short, frequent bouts (2–5 min, 3–5×/day) beat long single sessions. Expect mild symptom provocation that settles within ~20 minutes — that is therapeutic, not harmful.
Cervical rehabilitation
  • Manual therapy — segmental mobilisation of the upper cervical spine (C0–C3), sub-occipital and cervicothoracic soft-tissue work. Reproduction and then abolition of the habitual headache is the diagnostic-therapeutic loop.
  • Motor control — cranio-cervical flexion training (deep neck flexors), scapular setting, endurance before load.
  • Proprioceptive retraining — joint position error retraining with a laser pointer, then eye–head coordination and gaze stability with cervical movement.
  • Integration — combine cervical and oculomotor tasks, since real-world provocation involves both.
  • Sequencing: a retrospective cohort found that treating cervical dysfunction and BPPV in the first three weeks, then integrating vision and vestibular therapy, produced broad improvement across PCSS, DHI, CISS and BESS.61
Oculomotor and vergence therapy
  • Convergence — pencil push-ups, Brock string, jump vergence; measured by NPC break/recovery.
  • Saccades and pursuit — near–far Hart chart, metronome-paced saccades, pursuit with increasing amplitude and speed.
  • Accommodative facility — ±2.00 flippers, monocular then binocular.
  • Visual-vestibular integration — reading during head movement, optic flow exposure, busy-environment desensitisation.
  • Refer to neuro-optometry or orthoptics where symptoms persist beyond 6–8 weeks of therapy.
Autonomic / exercise-intolerance rehabilitation
  • Establish HRt with BCTT/BCBT; prescribe at 80–90% HRt, 20 min daily, retest weekly.
  • Where orthostatic intolerance dominates, begin recumbent (rowing, recumbent bike, swimming) and progress to upright.
  • Fluid 2–3 L/day and sodium liberalisation, compression garments, avoid prolonged standing and hot showers.
  • Add isometric lower-limb and core strengthening to support venous return.
  • Track heart-rate recovery and Δ HR as objective progress markers alongside symptoms.63

What rehabilitation actually looks like

It is usually not "brain training". It is: hands-on treatment and exercises for a stiff, irritable neck; specific eye-and-head movement exercises that retrain your balance system; graded exercise that rebuilds your tolerance to exertion; and practical strategies for sleep, pacing and stress. Most people need two or three of these, not all of them — which is why a proper assessment comes first.

Feeling mildly worse during an exercise and better within about 20 minutes is normal and expected. That is how the system retrains.

Symptom-specific management

Headache

First, classify it: migrainous, cervicogenic, tension-type, occipital neuralgia, or medication-overuse. These have different treatments and frequently coexist.

  • Non-pharmacological first line: sleep regularisation, hydration, regular meals, aerobic exercise, cervical treatment where cervicogenic, screen/lighting management, trigger diary.
  • Acute pharmacotherapy: simple analgesia early and adequately dosed. Limit analgesic use to <10–15 days per month to prevent medication-overuse headache — a very common and entirely iatrogenic driver of "persisting concussion symptoms".
  • Preventive options where headache is frequent and disabling: amitriptyline (also aids sleep), propranolol (caution in athletes and asthma), topiramate (cognitive side effects are problematic here), riboflavin/magnesium. Evidence in post-traumatic headache is largely extrapolated from primary headache disorders.
  • Interventional: greater occipital nerve block can be effective for occipital neuralgia and cervicogenic patterns. rTMS has preliminary evidence for post-traumatic headache.50,73
  • Avoid opioids and benzodiazepines.
Sleep disturbance
  • Sleep disturbance is independently associated with slower recovery — treat it as a target, not a symptom to wait out.22
  • Fixed wake time; limit naps to ≤30 min before mid-afternoon; light exposure in the morning; reduce evening screens; caffeine cut-off by early afternoon.
  • Melatonin is commonly used and low-risk, though a paediatric RCT found no benefit over placebo for persisting physical symptoms.73 CBT-I is the evidence-based treatment for insomnia persisting beyond 2–4 weeks. Screen for obstructive sleep apnoea in the appropriate phenotype.
Fatigue and cognitive fog
  • Pacing with a quota, not with symptoms. Set a sustainable activity level and increase it on a schedule rather than reacting to how each day feels — symptom-contingent pacing entrenches boom-and-bust cycles.
  • Schedule demanding cognitive work at the individual's best time of day; protect it with real breaks (not screen breaks).
  • Treat the contributors: sleep, mood, anaemia, thyroid, deconditioning, medication side effects.
  • Cognitive training and psychoeducation have supportive evidence for persisting symptoms; evidence for methylphenidate is limited.50
Mood, anxiety and fear of re-injury
  • Screen formally and repeatedly (GAD-7, PHQ-9). Roughly 20% of students report new anxiety or depressive symptoms after sport-related concussion.48
  • A high early mood-symptom burden raises PPCS risk — and identifies the group with the greatest benefit from prescribed aerobic exercise (HR 0.29).48
  • CBT, graded exposure to feared activity, and behavioural activation have supportive evidence.50
  • Address fear of re-injury explicitly before return to contact — it is a common, unspoken reason for underperformance and prolonged absence.
Dizziness and motion sensitivity
  • Differentiate BPPV (brief, positional), cervicogenic (neck-related, no nystagmus), central vestibular, orthostatic, and persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD).
  • PPPD is common after concussion, driven by visual dependence and hypervigilance, and responds to vestibular rehabilitation combined with CBT and sometimes SSRIs — not to vestibular suppressants.62
  • Avoid long-term vestibular sedatives (e.g. betahistine, prochlorperazine) — they block the central compensation you are trying to drive.

Managing symptoms day to day

  • Headache: keep a regular sleep and meal pattern, stay hydrated, and use simple painkillers fewer than 10–15 days a month — taking them more often than that causes a headache of its own.
  • Tiredness and fog: plan your activity by the clock, not by how you feel. Decide on a sustainable amount and increase it on a schedule, rather than doing too much on good days and collapsing on bad ones.
  • Dizziness: avoiding movement makes it worse. Graded, repeated exposure to the movements that bother you is what retrains the system.
  • Light and noise: adjust the environment first (screen brightness, warmer lighting, quieter rooms), then build tolerance back up. Wearing dark glasses indoors all day makes sensitivity worse over time.
  • Sleep: fix your wake-up time, keep naps short and early, and get morning daylight. Poor sleep measurably slows recovery, so it is worth treating in its own right.
  • Mood: feeling low, irritable or anxious after a concussion is common and expected. It is also one of the things that responds best to treatment — say so rather than waiting it out.
Light and noise sensitivity
  • Graded exposure, not avoidance. Continuous dark-glasses use indoors worsens photophobia over time by increasing photoreceptor sensitivity.
  • Adjust the environment first (screen brightness, warm lighting, matte surfaces, background noise reduction), then progressively re-expose.
  • FL-41 tinted lenses have some evidence in photophobia and are preferable to dark sunglasses indoors.

Persisting symptoms after concussion

Symptoms persisting beyond about 4 weeks in children or 2 weeks in adults are not a single disease. They are a final common pathway reached by several different routes — and each route has a different treatment.

The definitional problem — and why it matters

Reported prevalence ranges from 5% to 80% depending on the criteria, population and setting. Emergency-department cohorts and specialist rehabilitation cohorts are not the same people: treatment-seeking patients had higher symptom scores (RPQ 30 vs 17), worse functional outcome, and 43% vs 18% had not returned to work or school. Evidence generated in ED-recruited mTBI research may not generalise to the patients you see in a persisting-symptoms clinic.19 Amsterdam explicitly notes that no test or measure other than validated symptom rating scales is valid for diagnosing persisting symptoms.1,5

Multidisciplinary symptom-targeted treatment works

In a 2026 randomised trial of 158 young people (8–18) with persisting symptoms, an individualised multidisciplinary programme ("Concussion Essentials" — education, psychology and physiotherapy modules for up to 8 weeks) produced full recovery in 62.5% vs 37.0% of usual care, with post-treatment symptom scores of 8.5 vs 21.8 (p<0.0001).56 This is consistent with meta-analytic evidence that individually tailored multimodal intervention outperforms both rest and single-modality treatment.49

A sobering counterweight

In an observational cohort of adults with persisting symptoms, 83% received one or more treatments and rated them helpful — yet symptom change over 3 months was not associated with whether they were in treatment or with how many treatments they received.88 Perceived helpfulness is not efficacy. Specify the active ingredient, dose it, and measure the outcome.

Why hiding symptoms is the worst move available to you

Under-reporting is the single biggest failure point in concussion management. The maths is not close: an unreported concussion costs you a few weeks if you play on and get away with it, and can cost a season — or a career — if you do not.

Playing while concussed also makes a second injury more likely, makes recovery slower, and raises your risk of a muscle or ligament injury for months afterwards. Reporting early and doing the graded return properly is the fastest route back onto the field, not the slowest.

When you are still out after a month

Long recoveries are rarely one thing. Usually it is some combination of a neck that never settled, a balance system that has not recalibrated, lost fitness, disrupted sleep, and the entirely reasonable stress of being away from your sport and your team.

Two things matter most here. First, get assessed by someone who will identify which of those apply to you, because each has a different treatment. Second, stay connected to the squad — attend sessions, travel with the team, keep the routine. Isolation and loss of athletic identity genuinely slow recovery, and they are the part nobody schedules an appointment for.

If your symptoms have lasted longer than expected

This is common, it is not a sign that your brain is permanently damaged, and it is treatable. Persisting symptoms usually have several contributors at once — a stiff neck, a balance system that hasn't recalibrated, poor sleep, reduced fitness, and understandable worry about what's happening.

What helps most: a proper assessment that identifies which of these apply to you, a graded exercise programme, treatment aimed at your specific problems, and a clear explanation of what is going on. What helps least: waiting to feel better before doing anything.

How to use this clinically

Risk factors identify who needs earlier, more active and more multidisciplinary care — not who needs more rest. The highest-risk groups (high symptom burden, mood symptoms, vestibular impairment, exercise intolerance) are precisely the groups with the largest demonstrated treatment effects.48,52,42 Treat risk with intervention, not with caution.

Biopsychosocial and cognitive factors

The biological injury initiates the problem. What determines whether it resolves in two weeks or two years is frequently not biological at all.

Cognitive model: how symptoms are maintained

The maintenance cycle is well characterised and directly modifiable:

  1. Injury produces genuine physiological symptoms.
  2. Attribution — the patient (often reinforced by a clinician, a coach or the internet) attributes every subsequent normal sensation to brain damage.
  3. Hypervigilance — attention is directed inward; ordinary fatigue, ordinary headaches and ordinary forgetting are now detected and counted.
  4. Catastrophising and fear-avoidance — activity is reduced to avoid provoking symptoms.
  5. Deconditioning, sleep disruption, social withdrawal, low mood — which generate more of the same symptoms.
  6. The loop closes and confirms the belief.

This is why "good clinical explanation" is an intervention, and why symptom-contingent rest is iatrogenic. It is also why a functional overlay model of persisting symptoms — in which the initial injury destabilises networks with pre-existing vulnerability and the resulting pattern is perpetuated by non-specific factors — fits the clinical data better than a purely structural account.20,16

Regulatory systems: autonomic, immune and endocrine

Conditions that overlap symptomatically with persisting concussion — anxiety, depression, insomnia, chronic pain, chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia, cervical strain — all share disturbed autonomic function and its interactions with the immune and endocrine systems. A practical treatment framework built on this observation prioritises: sleep stabilisation, fatigue management, graded physical exercise, nutrition, relaxation and breathing protocols, and behavioural activation — the foundational, unglamorous interventions that stabilise regulatory systems.66

What to actually say to the patient

  • "Your scan is normal, and that is expected — this injury doesn't show on scans. It doesn't mean nothing happened."
  • "Most people recover fully. The strongest predictor of a good outcome is getting back to activity in graded steps, starting now."
  • "Some symptoms during exercise are expected and safe. Mild and settling within an hour is fine. That's how the system retrains."
  • "You are not damaged. Several different systems are out of tune, and each one has a treatment."
  • Avoid: "you'll just have to wait it out", "rest until you're symptom-free", "you may never be the same", and any unprompted mention of CTE.

Why worry makes symptoms worse — and what to do about it

After a concussion it's natural to monitor yourself closely. The problem is that everyone gets headaches, forgets things and feels tired sometimes. When you're watching for symptoms, you notice all of these — and each one feels like evidence that something is wrong. That worry raises muscle tension, disturbs sleep and leads to doing less, which produces more of the same symptoms.

Breaking that cycle is a real, evidence-based part of recovery: gradually resuming normal activity even when symptoms are mild, keeping a routine, staying socially connected, and getting a clear explanation of what's happening from someone who knows the evidence.

Repetitive head impacts, CTE and retirement decisions

How to hold this evidence honestly

Two things are true simultaneously. (1) There is a robust, dose-dependent relationship between cumulative repetitive head impact exposure and chronic traumatic encephalopathy pathology, and elevated neurodegenerative disease risk in former elite contact athletes. (2) The prevalence figures from brain banks are massively inflated by selection bias, CTE cannot be diagnosed in a living person, and most people who play contact sport will not develop it. Communicating only (1) causes real harm; communicating only (2) is not honest.

What the evidence does not support

  • The often-quoted "over half of contact athletes have CTE". A meta-analysis of 1,000 neuropathologically examined donors gave a pooled prevalence of 53.7% — but these are brain-bank donors, overwhelmingly recruited because families noticed symptoms, with a 39% suicide rate in the CTE group.70 This is not a population prevalence and must never be presented to a patient as one.
  • Diagnosis during life. There is no validated in-vivo biomarker. Tau PET tracers with adequate affinity for the 3R/4R tangles of CTE have not been identified; amyloid PET is typically negative. Traumatic encephalopathy syndrome criteria are research criteria under validation.30,87
  • Inevitability. In 200 former elite rugby players with brain health concerns (a self-selected, symptomatic group), cognitive testing did not differ significantly from controls, no player met criteria for dementia, 24 met TES research criteria, and provisional CTE certainty was "suggestive" in 21 and "probable/definite" in none.84
  • That a single concussion causes CTE. The exposure variable is cumulative repetitive head impacts, most of them non-concussive.23,83

Retirement from contact sport — how to approach the conversation

There is no evidence-based concussion count that mandates retirement. Amsterdam 2023 explicitly frames retirement as an individualised, shared decision. Factors that legitimately weigh in:

  • Decreasing force required to produce concussion, or increasing symptom severity and duration with each event.
  • Persisting symptoms or objective deficits that have not resolved with high-quality targeted rehabilitation.
  • Structural findings — contusion, haemorrhage, cavum septum pellucidum, congenital anomaly (Chiari, arachnoid cyst), or cervical instability.
  • Post-traumatic epilepsy; a second-impact event; prolonged loss of consciousness.
  • The athlete's own values, alternative career prospects, and access to non-contact participation.
Frame it as exposure reduction, not a binary

Options between "keep playing" and "retire" are often better than either: changing position, moving to a lower-contact code or level, ending the season rather than the career, reducing contact in training (which is where most exposure accumulates), and improving technique and neck strength. Discuss loss of identity, income and social structure explicitly — these drive non-disclosure of symptoms more than any medical factor.

If you're worried about long-term effects

The risk relates to total career exposure to head impacts — including the many small ones that never caused symptoms — far more than to any single concussion. The strongest things you can do are: report symptoms honestly and take the time off, reduce contact in training (where most impacts happen), and follow the return-to-play steps properly. The high percentages you may have seen in the news come from brains donated by families who were already concerned about symptoms — they are not the risk for an average player.

Children, female athletes, para athletes and non-athletes

Children and adolescents
  • Longer expected recovery — up to about 4 weeks, versus ~2 weeks in adults. Do not apply adult timelines.80
  • Age-specific tools: Child SCAT6 (8–12), Child SCOAT6 (8–12), SCAT6/SCOAT6 (13+), VOMS-Child for ages 5–9 using binary symptom reporting.81
  • Parent and child symptom scales both matter — parent-report captures behavioural change the child cannot articulate. The PCSI is the paediatric standard.
  • Return to learn takes priority over return to sport, and academic adjustments should be arranged early and tapered deliberately.
  • Underdiagnosis is substantial — in a matched cohort, 25.3% of children attending an ED with mTBI had persisting symptoms 6–60 months later, versus 2.4% of controls with distal radius fracture.68
  • Suboptimal effort is common in youth testing; anchor decisions in validated functional assessments rather than computerised scores alone.81
  • Developing brain considerations: higher head-to-body mass ratio, weaker cervical musculature, incomplete myelination, and greater vulnerability to diffuse swelling — the rationale for stricter contact policy in youth sport.14
Female athletes and women
  • Higher concussion rates than males in nearly every sex-comparable sport (lacrosse is the exception), and higher symptom burden.31
  • Slower recovery in several cohorts; female sex is an independent predictor of persisting symptoms in children (OR 1.20).67
  • Plausible contributors: lower neck strength and girth relative to head mass, differences in cerebral blood flow and regional glucose metabolism, neuroinflammatory and connectivity differences, hormonal phase effects, and higher symptom-reporting rates.89
  • Exercise prescription may need titrating down. Females achieved higher initial heart-rate thresholds, and in a pilot RCT those on high-volume exercise appeared to recover more slowly than those on low volume; 88% of exercise-intolerant females had to curtail a bout early vs 17% of males.47
  • The evidence base is thin. Only 37% of studies in a dedicated female-athlete prevention review reported female-specific estimates. Headgear in adolescent female lacrosse and soccer showed 30% lower concussion rates (IRR 0.70, very-low certainty); eyewear showed no protective effect.75
Para athletes
  • Amsterdam 2023 made the para athlete an explicit focus for the first time — but the evidence base remains sparse.1
  • Baseline is essential here, precisely where it is optional elsewhere: pre-existing visual, vestibular, motor or cognitive impairment makes population norms meaningless.
  • Balance and gait components of the SCAT6 may be unperformable or uninterpretable; symptom scales and vestibulo-ocular items may need modification.
  • Consider condition-specific confounders — spinal cord injury and autonomic dysreflexia, cerebral palsy and baseline motor variability, visual impairment and oculomotor testing, intellectual impairment and symptom reporting.
  • Document an individualised baseline and an individualised return-to-sport pathway in advance of the season, not after an injury.
Non-athletes and the general adult population
  • Most consensus evidence comes from adolescent and collegiate athletes. Community adults are older, more likely injured by falls or road traffic, more comorbid, and less physically active at baseline.21
  • Nearly half of adults referred to outpatient rehabilitation for persisting symptoms had a minimal head injury (no LOC, no PTA, normal imaging) — yet symptom burden and functional limitation were comparable to those with formally defined mTBI.19
  • BCTT interpretation must account for deconditioning, BMI, anxiety and non-injury symptoms; healthy sedentary controls also stop early.39,43
  • Supervised initiation of aerobic exercise still outperformed standard advice in a general adult population, although individualised prescription added no further benefit in that (underpowered) sample.55
  • Return to work is the analogue of return to learn — graded hours, task modification, and a defined review date.
  • New national guidance for adults now exists outside sport (e.g. Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand, 2025), emphasising early assessment, active recovery and primary-care tools.82

What a coach can actually change this season
  1. Cut contact in training. This is where most head impacts accumulate and it is entirely within your control. It has the best evidence of anything on this list.
  2. Mandate mouthguards. Cheap, high compliance, meaningful effect.
  3. Run a neuromuscular warm-up every session — the same programmes that reduce ACL and ankle injury also reduce concussion in rugby.
  4. Coach the technique — tackle height, head placement, heads-up contact.
  5. Build a culture where reporting is rewarded. Under-reporting is the largest single failure point in concussion management, and it is set by what coaches say and do, not by policy documents.
Emerging: instrumented mouthguards

iMGs give validated in-vivo measurement of head acceleration events and are being used to set contact-load thresholds and to trigger off-field assessment in elite rugby. They quantify exposure — the variable that actually drives long-term risk — rather than counting diagnosed concussions. They are not diagnostic devices, and no validated injury threshold exists.35,17
